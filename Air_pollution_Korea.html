Long-term effects of air pollution on mortality in people under 65, Korea

Keywords: air pollution, long-term exposure, cardiovascular mortality, cerebrovascular mortality, pneumonia, PCA, INLA, South Korea, Moran’s I, LISA, k-means

OverviewData & methodsCorrelation & PCAPollution mapsPollution profilesCardiovascularCerebrovascularPneumoniaReading the RRsLimitationsConclusion
Aim. To assess whether long-term exposure to five air pollutants is associated with deaths under age 65 from cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease and pneumonia across 250 Korean municipalities (2011–2019), and to map where pollution clusters.
Methods demonstrated. Negative binomial and Bayesian BYM models (R-INLA), PCA of pollutants, global and local Moran’s I, k-means pollution profiles. All code is in R, in the order it is run.
Key findings
  • Single-pollutant Bayesian models: CO, O₃ and PM10 were below 1 for cardiovascular deaths (RR 0.946, 0.970, 0.971).
  • Principal components (PC1–PC3) were above 1 for cardiovascular deaths; SO₂ and PC3 were above 1 for cerebrovascular deaths.
  • For pneumonia, CO and NO₂ were below 1 (0.936, 0.935) and PC1 above 1 (1.026).
  • Pollutant levels were strongly clustered in space (global Moran’s I 0.85–0.95). Of four pollution profiles, pneumonia deaths were higher in profiles 3 and 4 than in profile 1 (RR 1.22 and 1.41).
  • RRs below 1 for CO, NO₂, O₃ and PM10 are inverse associations. They are not read as a protective effect of these pollutants; see “Reading the relative risks below 1”.

This analysis links yearly municipality averages of five air pollutants (CO, NO₂, O₃, PM10, SO₂) to deaths under age 65 from cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease and pneumonia. Each pollutant was standardised, so a relative risk (RR) is the change in mortality for a one standard deviation increase. Models adjust for temperature, smoking, GRDP, financial independence, high-risk drinking, obesity, hypertension, sex ratio and population density, with log population under 65 as the offset.

Methods in brief

Overdispersion tests showed variance well above Poisson for all three outcomes (ratios 3.9, 3.6 and 4.8), so counts were modelled with a negative binomial distribution. Each pollutant was first fitted alone in a non-spatial negative binomial model, then in a Bayesian R-INLA model with a BYM spatial term, a municipality-specific time slope and a linear time trend. Because the pollutants are strongly correlated, a principal component analysis (PCA) was also run and its first three components were used as exposures.

Data and methods

The data set has 2,250 rows (250 municipalities × 9 years). The code below is run once at the top of the script: it reads the data, links each municipality to its 2018 Statistics Korea boundary (by minimising the distance between the CSV centroid and the polygon, because the polygon codes differ from SIG_CD), builds queen-contiguity neighbours (islands joined to the nearest area), and prepares the model data. Pollutants are standardised so that RRs are per 1 SD. If you have your own shapefile with SIG_CD, skip the linking step.

# Local Moran's I (LISA), 999 permutations, p < 0.05
lev <- c("Not significant", "High-High", "Low-Low", "Low-High", "High-Low")
lisa <- function(x) {
  z <- as.numeric(scale(x)); lag <- lag.listw(lw, z)
  li <- localmoran_perm(x, lw, nsim = 999); p <- li[, grep("^Pr.*Sim", colnames(li))[1]]
  factor(ifelse(p >= .05, lev[1], ifelse(z > 0 & lag > 0, lev[2], ifelse(z < 0 & lag < 0, lev[3],
         ifelse(z < 0 & lag > 0, lev[4], lev[5])))), levels = lev)
}

Cardiovascular mortality

The functions below fit each pollutant and each principal component on its own, first as a non-spatial negative binomial model and then, if R-INLA is installed, as a Bayesian BYM model with an area-specific time slope. The same call is repeated for the other two outcomes.

R code · Model functions (negative binomial and Bayesian BYM)
# One pollutant (or principal component) at a time: non-spatial negative binomial, and Bayesian BYM if R-INLA is installed
nb_rr <- function(y, x) {
  m <- glm.nb(as.formula(paste(y, "~", x, "+", f_cov, "+ offset(log(Popbelow65))")), data = su)
  c(exp(coef(m)[x]), exp(confint.default(m)[x, ]))                     # RR, 95% CI per 1 SD
}
bym_rr <- function(y, x) {
  f <- as.formula(paste(y, "~", x, "+", f_cov, "+ offset(log(Popbelow65)) +",
        "f(idarea, model = 'bym', graph = g) + f(SIG_CD1, Idtime, model = 'iid') + Idtime"))
  r <- inla(f, family = "nbinomial", data = su,
            control.compute = list(dic = TRUE, waic = TRUE), control.predictor = list(compute = TRUE))
  tm <- inla.tmarginal(function(z) exp(z), r$marginals.fixed[[x]])      # RR from the posterior
  inla.qmarginal(c(0.5, 0.025, 0.975), tm)                              # median, 95% CrI
}
run_all <- function(y, exposures = c(P, "PC1", "PC2", "PC3")) {
  out <- do.call(rbind, lapply(exposures, function(x) {
    n <- nb_rr(y, x); b <- if (has_inla) bym_rr(y, x) else rep(NA_real_, 3)
    data.frame(outcome = y, exposure = x, nb_rr = n[1], nb_lo = n[2], nb_hi = n[3],
               bym_rr = b[1], bym_lo = b[2], bym_hi = b[3])
  })); rownames(out) <- NULL; out
}

Reading the relative risks below 1

CO, NO₂, O₃ and PM10 are harmful pollutants. An RR below 1 here means that municipalities with higher levels had lower recorded death rates under 65 in this data set. It is not evidence that the pollutant lowers risk, and it is not interpreted that way anywhere on this page.

Why such associations can appear

  • Direction of PC1. In the PCA reported above, all PC1 loadings are negative, so a higher PC1 score means lower overall pollution. PC1 RRs above 1 (1.016 cardiovascular, 1.026 pneumonia) therefore describe higher mortality where overall pollution is lower. This is the same pattern as the single-pollutant RRs below 1.
  • Pollution tracks urban and rural areas. The pollutants are strongly correlated and spatially clustered (Moran’s I 0.86–0.95 for mean levels), and profile 3, with the highest CO and NO₂, lies around the capital region on the map. Urban and rural municipalities differ in age structure within the under-65 group, health-care access, lifestyle and how deaths are diagnosed and certified. The covariates only partly capture this.
  • Spatial confounding. The estimates moved toward 1 when the BYM spatial term was added (for example CO and cardiovascular deaths from 0.901 to 0.946). This suggests that part of the inverse signal comes from spatially structured differences that the models only partly absorb.
  • Ecological design. Municipality averages are used, and the offset is the total population under 65 without age standardisation.
  • Positive estimates. SO₂ (cerebrovascular RR 1.022) and PC3, which is driven by SO₂ (cardiovascular 1.082, cerebrovascular 1.058), were above 1 in the Bayesian models, in the direction expected for a harmful pollutant.

How the results are reported

RRs below 1 are called inverse associations with their intervals, and are not described as protective or as causal in either direction. Results are interpreted together with the principal components. Useful next steps would be age-standardised rates, an urban–rural or population-density stratification, lagged exposures, within-municipality (over-time) comparisons, and checking residuals for remaining spatial correlation.

Limitations

  • Ecological design. Areas are compared, not individuals, so the results do not show cause and effect.
  • Inverse associations. RRs below 1 for CO, NO₂, O₃ and PM10 most likely reflect confounding and the spatial structure of the data (see “Reading the relative risks below 1”).
  • Correlated pollutants. Single-pollutant and principal-component models separate individual effects poorly. The pollution profiles are descriptive.
  • Cluster maps. They use mean levels, queen weights and 999 permutations without correction for multiple testing. Boundaries are from 2018, and some municipalities changed over the period.
  • Exposure. Municipality averages say little about personal exposure, and nine years is a short window for long-term effects.

Conclusion

Significant results in the Bayesian (BYM) models

  • Cardiovascular: inverse associations with CO (RR 0.946), O₃ (0.970) and PM10 (0.971) per 1 SD, which are not interpreted as protective. Higher mortality with PC1 (1.016, where a higher score means lower overall pollution), PC2 (1.054) and PC3 (1.082, driven by SO₂).
  • Cerebrovascular: higher mortality with SO₂ (1.022) and PC3 (1.058). No other pollutant or component was significant.
  • Pneumonia (under 65): inverse associations with CO (0.936) and NO₂ (0.935), again not read as protective. Higher mortality with PC1 (1.026), a higher score meaning lower overall pollution.
  • NO₂ and SO₂ for cardiovascular deaths, and O₃, PM10 and SO₂ for pneumonia, had intervals that included 1.

Pollution maps and profiles

  • Pollutant levels were strongly clustered in space (global Moran’s I 0.85–0.95), and LISA maps show separate high and low clusters for each pollutant.
  • Four pollution profiles were identified. Pneumonia deaths were higher in profiles 3 and 4 than in profile 1 (RR 1.22 and 1.41).
  • RRs below 1 are treated as inverse associations that probably reflect confounding, not as protective effects.

Other points

  • PC1 explains 78% of pollutant variance and all its loadings are negative, so a higher PC1 score means lower overall pollution.
  • In the non-spatial negative binomial models, CO, NO₂, O₃ and PM10 were below 1 for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular deaths, and SO₂ was not significant.
