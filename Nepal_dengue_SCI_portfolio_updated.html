Nepal dengue and the Suitable Conditions Index (SCI), 2022–2024

Keywords: dengue, Suitable Conditions Index, Aedes aegypti, Aedes albopictus, spatiotemporal analysis, INLA, Nepal

OverviewData & methodsDescriptivePrimary resultLag patternDistrict levelLimitationsConclusion
Aim. To test whether the SCI, the percentage of days with temperature inside the range each Aedes species tolerates, is associated with monthly dengue incidence across the 77 districts of Nepal. A high SCI means the climate allows transmission; it does not show that mosquitoes are present.
Key findings
  • Within the same district, a 10-percentage-point higher SCI one month earlier was associated with about 5–6% more dengue (rate ratio 1.05–1.06; every interval above 1 across the main model and robustness checks).
  • The association appears about 1–4 months after exposure and is strongest at months 2–3.
  • Adding SCI improved model fit (WAIC) for Ae. albopictus but not clearly for Ae. aegypti.
  • The association was clearest in high-elevation districts (exploratory). These are associations in observational, aggregated data and do not show causation.

Data and methods

Data. Monthly dengue cases for all 77 districts, January 2022 to December 2024 (2,772 district-months), combined with meteorological, topographic, demographic and satellite-derived environmental data.

SCI. The share of days (%) in a district and month when daily minimum and maximum temperature both fall in the suitable range for each vector (Ae. aegypti 17.05–34.61 °C; Ae. albopictus 15.84–31.51 °C).

Primary model. Bayesian negative binomial model (R-INLA) for monthly district cases with a log-population offset. Exposure is lag-1 SCI as the within-district deviation from the district mean. Adjusted for district mean SCI, log elevation, log population density and lag-1 rainfall (linear and squared), with a BYM2 spatial effect, a cyclic month effect, a time trend and an AR(1) process within each district. Rate ratios (RR) are per +10 percentage points of SCI, with 95% credible intervals (CrI). Lag 1 was the pre-specified lag; distributed-lag and single-lag models examined lags 0–6.

Distributed-lag model, linear in exposure with a natural spline across lags. Cumulative effects are imprecise and their intervals include 1. WAIC is not informative for these models, so the pattern of effects should be used. A separate single-lag scan, fitting one model per lag, gave the best fit at lag 3 (Ae. aegypti) and lag 2 (Ae. albopictus), supporting a delayed association.

Limitations

  • Ecological, aggregated data over 36 months and 77 districts; surveillance reporting may vary by place and year.
  • SCI is temperature-only in this analysis and strongly related to elevation, so it partly acts as an elevation proxy; district-level adjusted estimates are inflated and should be interpreted with caution.
  • WAIC improved for Ae. albopictus but not clearly for Ae. aegypti, so SCI adds explanatory power beyond season, trend and spatial structure for one vector only.
  • Dengue incidence fell from 2022 to 2024, so year-level confounding is possible even with trend terms. Some residual spatial structure remains.

Conclusion

Higher temperature-based suitability one to four months earlier was associated with a small but consistent increase in dengue incidence within districts of Nepal, strongest at two to three months. The result held across robustness checks, but it reflects association in aggregated data, and the evidence for model improvement is stronger for Ae. albopictus than for Ae. aegypti.
