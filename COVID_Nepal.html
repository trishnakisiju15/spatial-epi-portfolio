Spatial epidemiology of COVID-19 incidence and its determinants in Nepal

Keywords: COVID-19, spatial epidemiology, Moran’s I, Bayesian hierarchical model, INLA, BYM, Nepal, relative risk

OverviewData & methodsDescriptiveModel resultsRegressionSpatial clusteringBayesian modelLimitationsConclusion
Aim. To describe the spatial distribution of COVID-19 incidence across Nepal’s 77 districts and to identify district-level factors associated with it.
Methods demonstrated. Negative binomial regression, spatial statistics (Moran’s I, local Moran, Getis-Ord Gi*), Bayesian hierarchical spatial modelling (R-INLA, BYM), model comparison and residual diagnostics.
Key findings
  • COVID-19 incidence is clustered (Moran’s I 0.341, p = 0.001). Getis-Ord found nine hot spots, all in Bagmati Province around Kathmandu, and no cold spots. Local Moran found one high–high cluster in the same area.
  • The BYM model with a PC prior fitted best (DIC 1353.3, against 1366.8 for the non-spatial model).
  • After spatial smoothing, higher incidence was associated with older median age (RR 1.85 per 1 SD), COPD (1.28), female obesity (1.21), household crowding (1.14) and population density (1.14).
  • Lower incidence was associated with a higher sex ratio (0.72) and better access to health facilities (0.85).
  • Male unemployment and temperature had intervals excluding 1 in the non-spatial model but not in the spatial model. Income inequality and hypertension were borderline in the non-spatial model and not significant in the spatial model.
  • Fitted counts track observed counts closely. The exception is Kathmandu, which is over-predicted (observed 333,784; fitted about 408,600).

How to read the results. An incidence rate ratio (IRR) or relative risk (RR) above 1 means higher incidence, and below 1 means lower incidence, for each 1 SD increase in a factor. The results compare districts and do not show cause and effect.

Data and methods

Outcome. This is an ecological, district-level analysis of the 77 districts of Nepal. The outcome is the district count of COVID-19 cases (COVID_cases2), modelled with the 2021 population as an offset; incidence (COVID_Inc2) is cases per 100,000 population. Cases were taken from the Ministry of Health and Population surveillance system for 23 January 2020 to 18 September 2022. Cases per district: median 4,037 (range 82–333,784). Population (2021): median 250,554 (range 5,645–2,017,532).

Covariates. Predictors cover socio-economic indicators, health-system access, comorbidity burden, climate and demographic covariates, taken from public sources (Table S1). Missing values were set to 0, and incidence and the covariates were converted to integers before analysis. Covariates were standardised (z-scores) for all regression models, so effects are reported per 1 SD.

Non-spatial models. Because incidence is strongly overdispersed, a negative binomial regression with a log-population offset was used. Each covariate was screened on its own (p < 0.25 to carry forward), collinearity was reduced using variance inflation factors (VIF), and the final model was chosen by backward elimination on AIC. Residuals were checked with DHARMa.

Spatial models. Spatial autocorrelation was tested with global Moran’s I, local Moran’s I (LISA) and Getis-Ord Gi*, using five nearest-neighbour weights. Bayesian spatial negative binomial models (non-spatial GLM, Besag and BYM) were fitted in R-INLA on a contiguity graph. Penalised-complexity (PC) priors were used for the BYM random effects.

Software. All analyses were done in R (glm.nb, DHARMa, spdep, R-INLA).

Overdispersion check

A Poisson overdispersion test on district incidence gives an observed-to-theoretical variance ratio of 2,909 (test statistic 221,088, p < 0.001). The data are strongly overdispersed, so negative binomial regression is used instead of Poisson.

Spatial clustering

Spatial distribution of incidence

District incidence is highest in the Kathmandu Valley and surrounding central districts, and low across the far west and east.

Neighbourhood structure and global Moran’s I

For Moran’s I, local Moran’s I and the Getis-Ord statistics, neighbours were defined as the five nearest district centroids (made symmetric), with a row-standardised weights matrix. Global Moran’s I was estimated by Monte Carlo simulation (999 permutations).

0.341global Moran’s I
0.001Monte Carlo p-value (smallest attainable with 999 permutations)

There is significant positive spatial autocorrelation: districts with high incidence tend to neighbour districts with high incidence.

Local Moran’s I (LISA)

Local Moran’s I identifies where the autocorrelation is concentrated. Districts were classified as high–high, low–low, or outliers (high–low, low–high) where the local statistic was significant at 5%. The only significant cluster is a high–high cluster in central Nepal around the Kathmandu Valley; no significant low–low clusters or spatial outliers were found.

R code · Neighbourhood, global and local Moran
pp <- knn2nb(knearneigh(coordinates(np3), k = 5), sym = TRUE)
wt <- nb2listw(pp, style = "W", zero.policy = TRUE)

moran.mc(np3@data$COVID_Inc2, listw = wt, nsim = 999, na.action = na.exclude)   # global Moran's I

local1 <- localmoran(x = np3@data$COVID_Inc2, listw = nb2listw(pp, style = "W"))
quadrant <- vector(mode = "numeric", length = nrow(local1))
m.qualification <- np3@data$COVID_Inc2 - mean(np3@data$COVID_Inc2)
m.local <- local1[, 1] - mean(local1[, 1])
quadrant[m.qualification > 0 & m.local > 0] <- 4      # high-high
quadrant[m.qualification < 0 & m.local < 0] <- 1      # low-low
quadrant[m.qualification < 0 & m.local > 0] <- 2      # low-high
quadrant[m.qualification > 0 & m.local < 0] <- 3      # high-low
quadrant[local1[, 5] > 0.05] <- 0                     # not significant

Hierarchical Bayesian spatial models (R-INLA)

Spatial structure was modelled with negative binomial models in R-INLA, using the covariates of the final regression model plus a district-level random effect. District adjacency was defined by shared borders (contiguity). Four models were compared: a non-spatial GLM, a Besag (ICAR) model, and a Besag–York–Mollié (BYM) model with either a penalised-complexity (PC) prior or a flat prior on the precisions. The PC prior on the standard deviations was set so that P(σ > 0.5) = 0.01, which shrinks the random effects towards zero unless the data support them.

R code · R-INLA models
library(INLA)
np2_nb <- poly2nb(np3)
nb2INLA("np2.graph", np2_nb)
np2.adj <- paste(getwd(), "/np2.graph", sep = "")
np3@data$idarea <- 1:nrow(np3@data)

hyperbym  <- list(prec.unstruct = list(prior = "pc.prec", param = c(0.5, 0.01)),
                  prec.spatial  = list(prior = "pc.prec", param = c(0.5, 0.01)))
hyper2bym <- list(list(prior = "flat", param = numeric()), list())

base <- COVID_cases2 ~ HH_crowding + Gini_coeff + p_unemployed_M + avg_temp1822 + Acc_to_healthfac +
        HTN_1920 + Obesity_F + COPD_1920 + Sex_ratio21 + Median_age + Pop_den21 + offset(log(Tot_pop21))
f0 <- base                                                                                    # GLM
f  <- update(base, . ~ . + f(idarea, model = "besag", graph = np2.adj, scale.model = TRUE))   # Besag
f1 <- update(base, . ~ . + f(idarea, model = "bym",   graph = np2.adj, scale.model = TRUE, hyper = hyperbym))   # BYM, PC prior
f2 <- update(base, . ~ . + f(idarea, model = "bym",   graph = np2.adj, scale.model = TRUE, hyper = hyper2bym))  # BYM, flat prior

fit <- function(fm) inla(fm, data = np2data, family = "nbinomial",
                         control.compute = list(dic = TRUE, waic = TRUE),
                         control.predictor = list(compute = TRUE))
res0 <- fit(f0); res <- fit(f); res1 <- fit(f1); res2 <- fit(f2)

# rate ratios from posterior marginals
tmarg <- inla.tmarginal(function(x) exp(x), res1$marginals.fixed$COPD_1920)
inla.qmarginal(c(0.025, 0.5, 0.975), tmarg)

After accounting for spatial structure, higher incidence remains associated with older median age, COPD burden, female obesity and household crowding, and lower incidence with a higher sex ratio and better access to health facilities. Population density has a weaker association (lower bound of the credible interval close to zero), while income inequality, male unemployment, temperature and hypertension have credible intervals that include zero.

Random effects and dispersion. Posterior mean precision of the unstructured (iid) component was 250 and of the spatially structured component 52; the posterior mean variance of the unstructured component was 0.012. The negative binomial size parameter (1/overdispersion) was 7.8 (95% CrI 4.4–11.0).

Model validation and fitted values

Fitted counts track observed counts closely across districts. The exception is Kathmandu, the district with by far the largest case count, which is over-predicted (observed 333,784; fitted about 408,600) and dominates the Pearson residual plot and the maps below.

Limitations

  • Ecological design. This study compares districts, not individuals, so the results do not show cause and effect or apply directly to individuals.
  • Severity. The analysis covers case incidence only; severity of disease was not considered.
  • Timing of covariates. Most covariates come from 2011–2021 surveys and registries and may not match the timing of the pandemic exactly. They are used as proxies, as large changes over this short period are unlikely.
  • Vaccination. Vaccination coverage was not available at district level, so it could not be included in these models.
  • Kathmandu. The BYM model over-predicts Kathmandu, the district with by far the highest case count.

Conclusion

  • Incidence is positively spatially autocorrelated (Moran’s I 0.341, p = 0.001). Local Moran found one high–high cluster around the Kathmandu Valley, and Getis-Ord found nine hot spots, all in Bagmati Province, with no cold spots.
  • The final negative binomial model kept 11 covariates; nine had a 95% CI excluding 1 (income inequality and hypertension were borderline). Older median age, COPD, female obesity, population density, household crowding, temperature and male unemployment went with higher incidence, and a higher sex ratio and better access to health facilities with lower incidence.
  • The BYM model with a PC prior fitted best (DIC 1353.3, against 1366.8 for the non-spatial model). After spatial smoothing, older median age (RR 1.85), COPD (1.28), female obesity (1.21), household crowding (1.14) and population density (1.14) stayed associated with higher incidence, and a higher sex ratio (0.72) and better access to health facilities (0.85) with lower incidence. Income inequality, male unemployment, temperature and hypertension were no longer significant.
  • Next steps: repeat the models separately for the pre- and post-vaccination periods.
