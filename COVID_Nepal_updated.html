Spatial epidemiology of COVID-19 incidence and its determinants in Nepal

Keywords: COVID-19, spatial epidemiology, Moran’s I, Bayesian hierarchical model, INLA, BYM2, Nepal, relative risk

OverviewData & methodsDescriptiveModel resultsRegressionSpatial clusteringBayesian modelLimitationsConclusion
Aim. To describe the spatial distribution of COVID-19 incidence across Nepal’s 77 districts and to identify district-level factors associated with it.
Methods demonstrated. Negative binomial regression, spatial statistics (Moran’s I, local Moran, Getis-Ord Gi*), Bayesian hierarchical spatial modelling (R-INLA, BYM2), model comparison and residual diagnostics.
Key findings
  • COVID-19 incidence is clustered (Moran’s I 0.483, p = 0.001, using shared-border neighbours). Local Moran found one high–high cluster of eight districts in central Nepal around the Kathmandu Valley. Getis-Ord Gi* found eight hot spots in the same area (four after Bonferroni adjustment for all 77 tests) and no cold spots.
  • The BYM2 model with PC priors had the lowest DIC and WAIC (DIC 1344.8, against 1365.9 for the non-spatial model). Models with an iid or Besag effect fitted no better than the non-spatial model.
  • After spatial smoothing, higher incidence was associated with diabetes cases (RR 1.27 per 1 SD), household crowding (1.17), female obesity (1.16), the income inequality indicator (1.14) and male unemployment (1.12).
  • Lower incidence was associated with better access to health facilities (0.85).
  • Temperature and hypertension had intervals that include 1 in the spatial model, although both had p < 0.05 in the non-spatial model.
  • The same pattern held with a Besag spatial effect in place of BYM2.

How to read the results. An incidence rate ratio (IRR) or relative risk (RR) above 1 means higher incidence, and below 1 means lower incidence, for each 1 SD increase in a factor. The results compare districts and do not show cause and effect.

Data and methods

Outcome. This is an ecological, district-level analysis of the 77 districts of Nepal. The outcome is the district count of COVID-19 cases (COVID_cases2), modelled with the 2021 population (in units of 100,000) as an offset; incidence (COVID_Inc2) is cases per 100,000 population. Cases were taken from the Ministry of Health and Population surveillance system for 23 January 2020 to 18 September 2022. Cases per district: median 4,037 (range 82–333,784). Population (2021): median 250,554 (range 5,645–2,017,532).

Covariates. Predictors cover socio-economic indicators, health-system access, comorbidity burden, climate and demographic covariates, taken from public sources (Table S1). Covariates were used as recorded, without rounding to whole numbers. None of the 22 covariates had missing values, so nothing was imputed. Six covariates contain exact zeros (male unemployment 10 districts, male smoking 3, female smoking 3, access to health facilities 1, male obesity 30, female obesity 22); these were kept as real values. Covariates were standardised (z-scores) for all regression models, so effects are reported per 1 SD.

Non-spatial models. Because incidence is strongly overdispersed, negative binomial regression with a log-population offset was used. Each covariate was first screened on its own, adjusted for sex ratio, population density and median age. A full model with 18 covariates was then fitted, collinearity was checked with variance inflation factors (VIF), and the final model was chosen by backward elimination on AIC. Residuals were checked with DHARMa. These models are exploratory; the Bayesian spatial model is the one to report.

Spatial models. Neighbours were districts that share a border (rook contiguity), with row-standardised weights. The same neighbour graph was used for global Moran’s I (one-sided Monte Carlo test, 999 permutations, with Holm-adjusted p-values), local Moran’s I (LISA), Getis-Ord Gi* and the Bayesian models. Bayesian spatial negative binomial models (no random effect, iid, Besag and BYM2) were fitted in R-INLA. The BYM2 model used penalised-complexity (PC) priors: P(σ > 1) = 0.01 for the standard deviation of the district effect and P(φ < 0.5) = 2/3 for the share of the variance that is spatially structured. The 11 fixed effects are the covariates of the final negative binomial model.

Software. All analyses were done in R (glm.nb, DHARMa, spdep, R-INLA).

Overdispersion check

A Poisson model of district case counts with the population offset and the 18 candidate covariates gives a Pearson chi-square divided by its degrees of freedom of 1,058 (a value near 1 is expected for Poisson data). The mean district count is 12,977 and the variance is 1.54 × 109. The data are strongly overdispersed, so negative binomial regression is used instead of Poisson.

Spatial clustering

Spatial distribution of incidence

District incidence is highest in the Kathmandu Valley, with moderate values in a few other districts. More than half of the districts are below 2,000 cases per 100,000 (median 1,453; maximum 16,544).

Neighbourhood structure and global Moran’s I

Neighbours were districts that share a border (rook contiguity), with a row-standardised weights matrix. The graph has 376 links, an average of 4.9 neighbours per district (range 2–9), no district without neighbours, and one connected component. Global Moran’s I was estimated by Monte Carlo simulation (999 permutations, one-sided test for positive autocorrelation).

Local Moran’s I (LISA)

Local Moran’s I identifies where the autocorrelation is concentrated. Districts were classified from the standardised incidence and the average incidence of their neighbours (the Moran scatter-plot quadrants), keeping those with a local p-value of 0.05 or less. Eight districts form a high–high cluster in central Nepal around the Kathmandu Valley; the other 69 are not significant, and no low–low clusters or spatial outliers were found. With 77 tests and no adjustment, the LISA result is exploratory.

R code · Local Moran
x      <- np3@data$COVID_Inc2
local1 <- localmoran(x, listw = wt, zero.policy = TRUE)
p_loc  <- local1[, grep("^Pr", colnames(local1))[1]]
z      <- as.numeric(scale(x))
lag_z  <- lag.listw(wt, z, zero.policy = TRUE)
sig    <- p_loc <= 0.05
quadrant <- rep(0, length(x))
quadrant[z > 0 & lag_z > 0 & sig] <- 4   # high-high
quadrant[z < 0 & lag_z < 0 & sig] <- 1   # low-low
quadrant[z < 0 & lag_z > 0 & sig] <- 2   # low-high
quadrant[z > 0 & lag_z < 0 & sig] <- 3   # high-low

Getis-Ord Gi* hot spots (Table 7)

The Gi* statistic includes the district itself in its neighbourhood and uses the same weights. Two-sided p-values come from the z-scores and were adjusted in three ways. Eight districts are significant at 5%, unadjusted and after the local Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg adjustments; four remain after a Bonferroni correction for all 77 tests. The significant districts form one group in central Nepal around the Kathmandu Valley, and there are no cold spots (Figure 7, outlined districts are significant after the FDR adjustment).

Hierarchical Bayesian spatial models (R-INLA)

Spatial structure was modelled with negative binomial models in R-INLA, using the covariates of the final regression model plus a district-level random effect. District adjacency was defined by shared borders, the same graph as in the spatial statistics (Figure 3). Four models were compared: a model with no random effect, an iid district effect, a Besag (ICAR) effect, and a BYM2 model that splits the district effect into a spatially structured part and an unstructured part. The BYM2 model used PC priors, P(σ > 1) = 0.01 for the standard deviation and P(φ < 0.5) = 2/3 for the spatial share of the variance.

After accounting for spatial structure, higher incidence remains associated with diabetes cases (RR 1.27), household crowding (1.17), female obesity (1.16), the income inequality indicator (1.14) and male unemployment (1.12), and lower incidence with better access to health facilities (0.85). Temperature and hypertension have credible intervals that include 1. A sensitivity model with a Besag effect instead of BYM2 gave the same pattern: the same six covariates had intervals excluding 1, and temperature and hypertension did not.

Random effects and dispersion. The posterior mean precision of the district effect was 19.4 (95% CrI 5.7–51.9), which corresponds to a standard deviation of about 0.26 on the log scale (95% CrI 0.14–0.41). The spatial share of that variance (φ) had a posterior mean of 0.31 with a very wide interval (0.005–0.945), so the data do not clearly separate spatially structured from unstructured variation. The negative binomial size parameter (1/overdispersion) was 11.0 (95% CrI 4.7–21.2).

Model validation and fitted values

Fitted values were converted to rates per 100,000 so districts of different size can be compared. The root mean squared error of the fitted rate is 529 per 100,000. Fitted rates range from about 327 to 18,352 per 100,000 and follow the observed pattern, with the highest values in the Kathmandu Valley (Figure 9). Pearson residuals lie within about ±2 (Figure 10).

Limitations

  • Ecological design. This study compares districts, not individuals, so the results do not show cause and effect or apply directly to individuals.
  • Timing of covariates. Most covariates come from 2011–2021 surveys and registries and may not match the timing of the pandemic exactly. They are used as proxies, as large changes over this short period are unlikely.
  • Vaccination coverage. Vaccination coverage was not available at district level, so it could not be included in these models.

Conclusion

  • COVID-19 incidence is spatially clustered (Moran’s I 0.483, p = 0.001), with a high–high cluster and eight Getis-Ord hot spots around the Kathmandu Valley and no cold spots.
  • The BYM2 model with PC priors had the lowest DIC (1344.8, against 1365.9 for the non-spatial model). After spatial smoothing, diabetes cases (RR 1.27), household crowding (1.17), female obesity (1.16), income inequality (1.14) and male unemployment (1.12) were associated with higher incidence, and better access to health facilities (0.85) with lower incidence.
  • Next steps: repeat the models separately for the pre- and post-vaccination periods.
